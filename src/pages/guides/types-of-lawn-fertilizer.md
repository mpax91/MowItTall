Every bag of lawn food is mostly about nitrogen, the first number on the bag. What changes between types is **how fast that nitrogen shows up** and what else is mixed in. [How to read the three numbers](/blog/how-to-read-a-fertilizer-bag/).

## Slow-Release

Slow-release nitrogen is coated or built so it breaks down a little at a time. Moisture, warmth and soil microbes let it out over about 6 to 10 weeks.

- **Good:** steady growth instead of a big surge (so less mowing right after), much harder to burn the lawn, less washes away.
- **Not so good:** slower to green up, and it costs a bit more.
- **On the label:** "water insoluble nitrogen," "slowly available," "polymer-coated urea," "sulfur-coated urea" or "methylene urea." Half or more of the nitrogen in those forms = a slow-release bag.
- **Use it for:** most feedings. It's what I use on Memorial Day and Labor Day.

## Quick-Release

Quick-release nitrogen (urea, ammonium sulfate and friends) dissolves as soon as it's watered in.

- **Good:** green in days. Cheap.
- **Not so good:** burns the lawn if you overdo it or spread it on a hot day, fades in a few weeks, and gives a growth spurt you'll be mowing off.
- **Use it for:** a fast green-up before a party, or late fall once growth has stopped. [The pre-party plan](/blog/nitrogen-iron-lawn-before-party/).

Most lawn bags are a blend of both. That's fine. Just look for at least half slow-release.

## Granular vs. Liquid

**Granular** goes down with a spreader and feeds for weeks. **Liquid** goes on with a sprayer, works fast and fades fast. For homeowners, granular is easier to get even and lasts longer. [Granules vs. spray, honestly](/blog/granules-vs-spray/).

## Starter

Starter fertilizer has a big middle number (phosphorus) to help new roots. **Only use it when you seed or lay sod.** An established lawn rarely needs phosphorus unless a soil test says so.

## Organic

Natural organic fertilizers come from things like composted manure, feather meal or processed biosolids. Soil microbes have to break them down first, so they act like slow-release, and they work slower in cold soil.

- **Good:** very hard to burn the lawn, adds a little organic matter.
- **Not so good:** lower numbers on the bag, so it takes more pounds (and money) to put down the same nitrogen. Some smell.

## Weed and Feed

Fertilizer and weed killer in one bag. Sounds efficient. The problem: the best time to feed and the best time to kill weeds usually aren't the same day, and you end up spreading weed killer on the whole lawn when you have weeds in three spots. Spot-spray instead. And never use weed and feed where you're seeding. [Weed killer and grass seed](/guides/weed-killer-and-grass-seed/).

## Winterizer

Mostly a marketing name for a late-fall feeding, sometimes with extra potassium. A regular lawn fertilizer does the same job for the optional Thanksgiving feeding.

## Which One Should I Buy?

For most Northeast lawns: **a lawn fertilizer with a 0 in the middle and at least half slow-release nitrogen.** Starter only when seeding. Skip weed and feed. Then put it down on the holidays: [the holiday fertilizer schedule](/blog/holiday-lawn-fertilizer-schedule/).
