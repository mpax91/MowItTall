**Mow light leaves right into your lawn. When they pile up in October and November, blow or rake most of them off first, then mulch the rest.**

In Bedford, gas leaf blowers are banned, and people still have strong feelings about it. I’m not here to referee that fight. I’m here to tell you when you need to move leaves and when you don’t.

## Light Leaf Days: Mow Them

Run your mower over the leaves when they’re dry. A regular mower chops them into little flakes that fall down between the grass blades. Within a couple of weeks, they break down and feed the soil. ([My full leaf guide](/guides/should-i-mulch-leaves-into-lawn/) has the details.)

Free fertilizer. No blowing. No raking. No 47 paper bags at the curb.

## Leaf Avalanche: Blow or Rake First

Peak drop under a big maple or oak ([the worst leaf trees, ranked](/blog/worst-trees-for-fall-leaves/)) is more than any mower can chop. Try it and you’ll smother the grass and choke the deck. So blow or rake the bulk into the beds or out to the curb, then mulch what’s left. No shame in it. That’s just math.

## The Five-Second Test

After you mow, look down. **Can you see grass through the chopped leaves?** You’re good. Looks like a brown carpet? Mow again, or move some off.

- **Go when they’re dry.** Wet leaves clump and make a mess.
- **Go often.** A pass every week through the fall beats one heroic mow on Thanksgiving weekend.

## But the Neighbors Blow Theirs Into My Yard

Then thank them for the free fertilizer and mow it in. Then [do your Halloween prep](/blog/halloween-is-the-lawn-super-bowl/), because a leaf-free lawn in late October is the whole point.

Somewhere on this site there’s a gas leaf blower hiding in a hedge. Go ahead and find it. Bedford would like a word.
