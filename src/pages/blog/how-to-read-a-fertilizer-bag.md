**The three big numbers on a fertilizer bag are the percent of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, by weight.** So 24-0-6 means 24% nitrogen, 0% phosphorus, 6% potassium. Your lawn mostly wants the first one. That’s 80% of what you need to know.

The other 20% is where it gets weird. Like the fact that the second number is kind of lying to you. Or that most of the bag isn’t fertilizer at all.

Grab any bag and plug in its numbers. Watch what falls out.

## Wait, where’s the rest of the bag?

A 50 lb bag of 24-0-6 has 12 lb of nitrogen and 3 lb of potash. That’s 15 lb of the good stuff. The other 35 lb is what carries it.

Before you call the cops: that’s a good thing. Try spreading 12 lb of pure nitrogen evenly over a quarter acre. You can’t. You’d burn stripes into your lawn, and not the good kind. The carrier spreads it thin so every square foot gets a little.

## Meet N, P and K

**N is nitrogen: the gas pedal.** It makes grass green and makes it grow. That’s why it’s the biggest number on every lawn bag. Too much and you’re mowing twice a week and inviting disease. Fun fact: the nitrogen in fertilizer is basically pulled out of thin air. The air you’re breathing right now is mostly nitrogen. Somebody figured out how to bag it.

**P is phosphorus: the root guy.** It helps baby grass grow roots. A lawn that’s already established usually has plenty in the soil. That’s why most lawn food has a 0 in the middle. **Starter fertilizer** has a big middle number because it’s made for new seed.

**K is potassium: the trainer.** It helps grass handle heat, drought, cold and disease. It doesn’t make anything greener. It makes it tougher. Fall bags often bump this number up.

## Plot twist: the second number is lying to you

Okay, fibbing. Only the first number is the real thing. The second is phosphate and the third is potash. Those are phosphorus and potassium wearing heavy coats made of oxygen.

Only about 44% of the phosphate is actual phosphorus, and about 83% of the potash is actual potassium. So a bag that says 10 in the middle really has about 4.4% phosphorus.

Does this matter for your lawn? Barely. It’s just a great fact for a cookout. And now you know why the decoder shows “real P” and “real K.”

## Fast vs. slow: the most important line nobody reads

Under the big numbers there’s a box called the **Guaranteed Analysis.** Look for the words **“water insoluble nitrogen”** or **“slowly available nitrogen.”** That’s the slow-release part.

- **Fast-release** greens up in days, can burn the lawn if you overdo it, and is gone in a few weeks. A sugar rush.
- **Slow-release** feeds steadily for weeks. A real meal.

Here’s the rule: if more than half of the nitrogen is slow-release, it’s a slow-release fertilizer. Divide the slow number by the total nitrogen. Or type it into the decoder and let it do the math.

## Other stuff on the label

- **Iron (Fe).** Makes grass dark green fast without making it grow. A spray tan for your lawn.
- **Sulfur, magnesium and friends.** Small amounts. Nice to have, not the reason you’re buying the bag.
- **Weed and feed.** Fertilizer plus weed killer in one bag. Sounds efficient. The problem: the best time to feed and the best time to kill weeds usually aren’t the same day. One of the two jobs gets done badly.
- **Organic.** Low numbers like 6-2-0. Gentle, slow, hard to burn with. You’ll spread more of it, it costs more per pound of nitrogen, and some of it smells like a farm for a day.

## Name brand vs. store brand

Your grass can’t read. It has no idea what logo is on the bag.

Two bags with similar numbers do about the same job. A 26-0-4 and a 24-0-6 are basically twins. So what’s actually worth paying more for?

- **More slow-release nitrogen.** This is the big one.
- **Even granules.** Same size, not dusty. They spread evenly and don’t clog the spreader.
- **Extra iron,** if you want that fast dark green.

Everything else is the picture of the golf course on the front. Here’s the move: **compare price per pound of nitrogen, not price per bag.** Bag price divided by (bag weight × first number ÷ 100). Or put the price in the decoder. Pay for slow-release, not for the logo.

## How much to put down

About **1 pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet,** max, per feeding. Less for a light one. The spreader settings on the bag are built around this, so follow them. More is not better. More is mowing.

## The cheat sheet

- **Normal lawn food:** big first number, 0 in the middle, small last. Like 24-0-6.
- **New seed:** starter fertilizer. Big middle number.
- **Fall:** a bigger last number is a nice bonus.
- **10-10-10:** put it back. That’s for tomatoes.
- **Read the box:** more than half slow-release is what you want.
