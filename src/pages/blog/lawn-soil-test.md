**A soil test tells you your soil’s pH and which nutrients are low or high, so you stop buying stuff your lawn doesn’t need.** Do one when you’re starting out, then every few years after that. It’s cheap, it takes 20 minutes, and you mail a bag of dirt. That’s it.

Think of it as blood work for your lawn. You could keep guessing why you feel tired, or you could get the numbers and know.

## Why bother?

- **You stop guessing.** Most people throw down whatever the guy at the store says. Some of it your lawn needs. Some of it is money in the trash.
- **It saves money.** If your soil is already loaded with phosphorus or potassium, you can buy cheaper bags without it.
- **pH is the silent killer.** If your pH is off, the food is in the soil but your grass can’t get to it. You can fertilize all day and get nothing. Around here, soil tends to run acidic. A lot of “my lawn won’t green up” problems are really pH problems.
- **It finds stuff you can’t see.** Low magnesium. Salt from the road. You can’t eyeball that.
- **It gives you a starting line.** Test now, test again in a few years, and you’ll see if what you’re doing is working.

## How often?

**Once when you start. Then every few years.** Every 3 years is plenty for most lawns. Soil changes slowly. Testing every spring is like stepping on the scale every hour.

Two exceptions:

- **If you added lime to fix low pH,** test again about a year later to see if it worked.
- **If something weird is going on,** like a big patch that won’t grow no matter what, test that spot on its own.

Test at the same time of year and use the same lab each time. Different labs measure differently, so switching labs is like switching scales mid-diet.

## How to do it

This is the part people overthink. Don’t.

1. **Pick your timing.** Spring or fall, when the ground isn’t frozen or soaking wet. Wait a couple of months after you fertilize or lime so you’re testing the soil, not last month’s bag.
2. **Get the gear.** A clean trowel or soil probe and a clean **plastic** bucket. Skip the old galvanized pail. It can add zinc to your sample, and then you’re testing the bucket.
3. **Zigzag the yard.** Take 10 to 15 small scoops from all over the lawn. More spots means a better average.
4. **Go 3 to 4 inches deep.** That’s where grass roots live. Pull off the grass and thatch on top. You want dirt, not lawn.
5. **Mix it up.** Dump everything in the bucket, break up the clumps and mix well. Spread it on newspaper to air dry if it’s wet.
6. **Fill the bag and mail it.** Fill to the line on the lab’s bag or box. Your county’s Cooperative Extension office can point you to a lab, or use a mail-in kit.

**Pro tip:** if your front and back yards are totally different (sunny vs. shady, old lawn vs. new), send them as two separate samples.

## How to read it

Your report will be a wall of numbers. Most of it doesn’t matter much. Read it in this order and stop when you get bored.

**1. pH.** The big one. It runs from 0 to 14, and grass likes 6.0 to 7.0.

- **Below 6:** sour (acidic). Common around here. Lime fixes it.
- **6.0 to 7.0:** the sweet spot. Leave it alone.
- **Above 7:** sweet (alkaline). Rare around here. Don’t lime.

Some reports also list a **buffer pH.** That’s how stubborn your soil is about changing. You don’t need to understand it. The lab uses it to tell you how much lime to put down.

**2. Phosphorus (P) and potassium (K).** These are the second and third numbers on a [fertilizer bag](/blog/how-to-read-a-fertilizer-bag/). Your report will say low, optimal or high. Low means add some. High means stop adding.

**3. Organic matter and CEC.** Organic matter is the old roots, clippings and leaves broken down in your soil. More is better. CEC is how big your soil’s pantry is. Sandy soil has a small pantry, so feed it lighter and more often. Clay has a big one.

**4. Everything else.** Calcium, magnesium, sulfur, iron, manganese, zinc, boron and friends. Here’s the secret: **fix pH first.** When pH is off, a lot of these read weird. Fix the pH and many of them sort themselves out.

**And the nitrogen number?** Mostly ignore it. Nitrogen moves around and changes week to week. Your lawn needs it every year no matter what the test says. That’s what the [holiday schedule](/blog/holiday-lawn-fertilizer-schedule/) is for.

**One more thing:** results come in parts per million (ppm) or pounds per acre, and every lab uses its own ranges. Compare your numbers to **your** lab’s “optimal” range, not something you saw on a forum.

## What to do about it

Here’s the playbook for the stuff that actually comes up.

**Low pH (below 6).** Lime. Your report should tell you how much. Never put down more than 50 pounds per 1,000 square feet at once. If it calls for more, split it between spring and fall. Pelletized lime goes through a regular spreader and is way less dusty. Be patient. Lime takes months to kick in.

**Calcitic or dolomitic?** Two kinds of lime. Both raise pH. Dolomitic adds magnesium too. **Low magnesium? Dolomitic. Otherwise, calcitic.** If magnesium is high, definitely calcitic.

**High pH (above 7).** Don’t lime. If it’s way high, call your Cooperative Extension office before you try to fix it. Overcorrecting is worse than waiting.

**Low phosphorus.** Use a fertilizer with a number in the middle for a season or two. If you’re seeding, starter fertilizer kills two birds.

**High phosphorus.** You can’t take nutrients out of the soil. Just stop adding them. Buy lawn food with a 0 in the middle and it drops on its own over a few years.

**Low potassium.** Pick a bag with a bigger last number, especially for fall.

**High potassium.** Skip the high-potassium fall bags. Not a big deal.

**High iron or manganese.** Usually a sign of acidic soil. Fix the pH and don’t add more.

**Low boron or other tiny stuff.** Don’t chase every micronutrient. If the lawn looks fine, it is fine. Some of these are harmful in big doses, so more is definitely not better.

**High sodium.** Usually road salt along the street or driveway. Water that strip deeply to flush it, and ask your extension office about gypsum.

**Low organic matter.** Leave your clippings. Mow over your leaves. After you aerate, rake in a thin layer of compost. Free fixes, mostly.

## The cheat sheet

- **Test once when you start, then every few years.**
- **10 to 15 spots, 3 to 4 inches deep, plastic bucket.**
- **Read pH first.** 6 to 7 is perfect.
- **Low? Add it. High? Stop adding it.**
- **Lime only if the test says so.** 50 pounds per 1,000 square feet, max, per round.
- **Ignore the nitrogen number.** Feed on the holidays.
